#### CHESTER

Sir,

Some years ago, you approached me with solicitations for my involvement in the arrangement of an office of address with regards to the new affairs you were considering for those you name your Invisible College. While I have long allowed myself such pride as to position myself a member of what I may dub the Hartlib Circle, you will recall I was less than receptive to these solicitations. I have little knowledge and still less interest in the practice of alchemy and found many of your suppositions regarding such contrary to my own philosophies. "Such things cannot exist," I recall were my words, and so I did not entangle myself in this strange new group you are assembling, most especially given the involvement of the Crown in this affair.

I am now of a mind to reverse that choice, due to an encounter of my own this past month.

I was visiting my friend and correspondent, a Mr M.K. Adder, the curate of a Gloucester parish, and had entailed by way of my return, a ferry over that wider bit of the Severn not yet bridged. My coach was much delayed in departing, the cause being some infirmity of the horses, and my arrival at the ferry house, at a far later hour than was agreed. As you may readily suppose, the promised ferry was absent, and my journey uncertain. You shall recall, sir, how thick the mists were across the county this season, and so too it was that day, mixed with the darkening sky so as to render me all but blind as I looked from the window of my coach.

Then, within the shadowed dusk came motion and the prow of a boat's sudden appearance. It was a vessel too large to be a mere ferry, yet it had but a single man to crew it, and him stood still and silent upon the deck, his cloak concealing the most of him. His arm was white as bone as he crooked his finger in a gesture of welcome.

I will confess, sir, a terror overcame me. A terror I could scarcely name, nor less articulate, yet was so true I felt my whole self begin to tremble as I sat watching.

A second time the ferryman beckoned me, but I had neither desire nor ability to follow him aboard, and with a quavering voice instructed my driver to remain.

It was then my attention was turned to a commotion behind me. Mine was, it seemed, not the only coach so delayed, and a gentleman of a singularly ruddy complexion was swearing the most vile oaths and demanding he be allowed first upon the boat. I was in no fit state to refuse him, and so he summarily boarded.

Immediately the vessel moved away, silent and strange, and I reckoned it clear that the boat had not been moored or tied upon the dock. It had been waiting.

A change then came upon the air which caused those horses that had so troubled my coach journey began to fight, one with another, and so with much ado I alighted to keep myself from harm. I saw as I went thither that the horses upon the quickly vanishing ferry had set about themselves with an even fiercer violence than those still on land. Their teeth tore into the flesh of their fellows, loosing blood that rained down upon the timbers in great torrents. The setting of the coach had placed the tormented beasts near the edge of the boat, but for all the blood that flew through the air not a drop of it reached the waters of the river. It flowed and fell rather onto the ferry, and the wood did seem to sup of it greedily.

I went to call out, to offer aid, but the fogs rolled in with such speed and thickness that my eyes were masked and my voice muffled up within a moment.

All was still for a time and then came the most awful cry ever to fall upon my ears. It was a man, doubt it not, and a true man, not whatever covered itself in a cloak and called itself a man. Most likely it was the surly passenger, but his voice was like the cries of the damned and accompanying it was a great tearing.

I stood still as though my very legs had taken root and my senses had abandoned me, listening to that terrible sound. Minutes passed before the silence returned and the mists cleared.

The ferry remained, as did its master. The coach sat there also, but the horses that had been so frenzied were gone, without a drop of blood to mark their passing. But still I avoid the heart of the matter, for in each of the ferryman's hands was a sheet, hanging limply. Not a sheet of cloth though, but a sheet of skin. One for the coach driver, and one for the passenger. I could still see the ruddy complexion of his face and -- it sickens me to think on it -- the hanging flesh that was once his lips still moved as if to scream.

You may imagine my horror, my awful terror at the sight. And as I felt it overtake me, I saw this inhuman figure turn and herald me as though in gratitude for that awful feeling before departing into the darkness of the night.

I am convinced in my judgment that what I witnessed was of that manner you described to me those years ago, animated by Dread and supped by our horrors. I would remain forever troubled in my conscience were I to know of such things and stand idle and so whatever need you have of me, I shall provide it, and whatever service I may render to your Invisible College or its successor society, it is yours.

I await your response with as stout a heart as I may muster.

Your dutiful servant,  
Samuel Pepys.
