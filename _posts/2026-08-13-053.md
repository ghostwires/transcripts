#### GERTRUDE

Where to start?

I first became aware of the organisation in the early '70s. At the time I was struggling to make sense of an experience I had had some years before. The details are, quite frankly, none of your business, but it had several important effects on me: one, it left me certain that monsters were very real in a supernatural sense, not merely a metaphorical one; two, it rendered me utterly alone in the world, without any remaining family, loved ones or meaningful connections; and three, it led to me taking my first two human lives.

You can perhaps imagine the effect such a radical change has on a person. Or perhaps you can't. Either way, the few years immediately afterwards were spent searching for the meaning of what happened, or at least an explanation. It should not be difficult to imagine how such a search might lead me to the doorstep of The Magnus Institute.

When I first came to them they were rude, dismissive and extremely skeptical. They did not bother to hide the fact that they viewed me as someone who, if not an outright fantasist, had stumbled across something she could not hope to understand.

The first time I visited, they accused me of wasting their time and had me escorted from the premises. It was this, more than anything else, that convinced me the Institute knew what they were talking about. Unlike the many other groups I had found that promised insight into the darker corners of the world, they were not recruiting or selling or reassuring. I was certain they knew what was going on and decided I would stop at nothing to join them and learn the truth for myself.

It was the third time that I broke into the Institute that I finally met Dr Welling. By this point I had found more than enough evidence of the strange and unnatural in that building, and there was no longer any pretense between us. Instead, I could see in his eyes something I had witnessed only once before: someone deciding whether or not to kill me. However, unlike the thing I had met at the bottom of my garden, Welling was too weak to make the decision.

He had me recite my story yet again, and paid particular attention to the point at which I had to murder my father and my sister. Then he was silent for some time.

"Do you think you could kill again?" he asked at last.

I said yes. It was strange; the question evoked no feeling in me at all. No fear, no eagerness, no revulsion or discomfort. The violence meant nothing to me at all and I realised in that moment that perhaps the thing in my garden won after all.

Dr Welling nodded and gestured for me to follow him. He led me from his small office, through austere wood-panelled corridors, and finally down some ancient stairs to a cold stone basement and there, surrounded by three worried-looking academics, I found my first project with them.

It was quite an easy one to start with. While it was still recognisably human, it was clearly in a great deal of pain flailing and spasming on the cold flagstones. A thin trickle of molten gold streamed from its mouth, ears and nose. There was even some below its fingernails and under its closed eyelids and the skin around these areas was cracked and blistered from the extreme heat. I would learn later that this was the first in their new series of experiments, and they hadn't been prepared for such a... grotesque result. The only thing they had available for me to use was a heavy iron spanner left behind by a workman who had been fixing the building's piping.

It was enough.

After the business was finished, there was no question about my membership of the Institute, or what my role within it was going to be. I still don't know if I ever fully believed in the Institute's goals, but I've considered the question for twenty-five years.

You're perhaps too young to understand how difficult it can be to remember how you truly felt about something so long ago. It becomes coloured, distorted... Everything you said, you did, you felt, it all bleeds into each other and gets muddled by the things that follows.

It certainly waxed and waned over the decades I worked for them. I did respect their mission, though I was perhaps more grounded, less utopian, than my colleagues; it was hard not to be when I was the one disposing of their many, many failures.

The messianic rhetoric always irritated me, but the fundamental idea -- investigating a force that transformed people for fear and evil, then finding a way to invert it, to transform someone for hope and light -- that made sense to me.

Perhaps I was naïve... No, I was definitely naïve, but I needed there to be at least a chance that something good could come from all that awfulness.

I don't know how many people I killed for the Magnus Institute over those three decades. More than twenty, certainly. They normally made one attempt each year, although there were exceptions for special circumstances.

Not all their failures were killed, of course. One escaped, a few were locked away, experimented on. They kept the Blood-Brewer for almost five years before having me dispose of him, and I think the Archivist was still down there when I burned the place down.

I didn't set the blaze personally, of course, but we all knew the O.I.A.R. was paying attention and I had little doubt what the outcome of my anonymous tip would be. I was surprised by how sloppy their hirelings were, though. It was messy work. Overdone with no eye for detail.

As for why I did it? You already met him. Gerry was nine when he underwent the first of the transmutation rituals. I was... out of favour at the time, and so my colleagues did not consult with me about the use of children. I didn't even realise they had done so until Gerry had already been changed into the being of raw positivity that I now protect.

I have never had children. I never wished to have children. I don't like children. But even I have my limits, and finding out that in their desperation for results, they had started to use them as subjects... I suppose it was all somewhat academic to them. When it all inevitably went wrong, they weren't the ones that would need to... dispose of the results.

Like me, Gerry had no family to speak of and he was already so... changed that he would need to be cared for by someone who understood. What else could I do?

I took him away, along with a few items from the vault that I could sell to some collectors for living costs.

I remember, I was smiling when I stole him from the Institute; I was smiling when I called the OIAR; and I was smiling when my colleagues were murdered and the building in which I had done such terrible things was burned to the ground.

I don't know how much of that was due to his presence. I suspect the point is moot. It was an odd experience regardless.
